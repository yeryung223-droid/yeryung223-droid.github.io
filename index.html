I study Korean Dance at Sungkyunkwan University and am interested in how audiences experience live performance — what creates satisfaction, what evokes emotion, and why people choose to attend in the first place.

Why Concert Tickets?

The live music industry has transformed dramatically in recent years. Global concert revenue surpassed $25 billion in 2023, with ticket prices rising sharply due to dynamic pricing models adopted by major platforms like Ticketmaster and StubHub. For fans, purchasing a ticket is no longer a simple transaction — it involves trade-offs between price, proximity, artist connection, and the fear of missing out.

Audience Satisfaction Research

Research in experience economy theory (Pine & Gilmore, 1999) argues that audiences do not just consume events — they seek memorable experiences. In performance studies, satisfaction is shaped by factors including seat quality, artist rapport, sound design, crowd atmosphere, and perceived value for money. Studies show that emotional connection to the performer often outweighs physical comfort when measuring post-concert satisfaction.

Fan Culture & Buying Behavior

K-pop fandoms have reshaped ticket purchasing behavior in Korea and globally. Dedicated fans routinely invest in VIP packages, fan meetings, and merchandise bundles — prioritizing proximity and exclusive access over cost efficiency. This phenomenon raises important questions: does higher spending lead to higher satisfaction, or does expectation inflation reduce enjoyment?

01

Introduction

Live concerts represent one of the most emotionally charged forms of entertainment. Unlike streaming or recorded media, a concert is a singular, unrepeatable event — creating conditions where audience expectations, physical environment, and performer energy converge in real time. This uniqueness makes the concert experience a particularly rich subject for audience satisfaction research.

In recent years, the concert ticketing landscape has undergone significant disruption. The widespread adoption of dynamic pricing — where ticket prices fluctuate based on demand, similar to airline tickets — has forced audiences to make complex value judgments. A floor-level ticket for a top-tier artist may cost $300 or more, while nosebleed seats at the same show might be available for $40. The question this raises is not simply economic: it is psychological. Does where you sit change how you feel?

This project investigates those questions by analyzing concert ticket purchasing patterns and their relationship to satisfaction outcomes — with a focus on young audiences in Korea, where fan culture and live performance are deeply intertwined.

02

Research Background

The global live music industry recovered dramatically after the pandemic, with concert revenues reaching record highs in 2023–2024. Ticketmaster reported over 620 million tickets sold globally in 2023, while artist tours by performers such as Taylor Swift and BTS generated billions in economic activity. This surge has been accompanied by growing public frustration over pricing transparency, hidden fees, and the difficulty of securing tickets at face value.

In Korea specifically, the K-pop concert market has developed its own ecosystem. Fan clubs (팬클럽) operate hierarchical ticketing priority systems, and fan engagement extends well beyond the concert itself — encompassing fan sign events, pre-show content, and post-concert streaming parties. This culture creates a spending environment where the ticket is often the minimum investment, not the maximum.

Academic research on live performance satisfaction draws from multiple disciplines. Pine & Gilmore's Experience Economy framework suggests that audiences increasingly seek transformative experiences over mere consumption. Meanwhile, Thaler's concept of mental accounting helps explain why a fan who pays $200 for a VIP ticket may report higher satisfaction than one who paid $60 for budget seats — not because the experience was objectively better, but because the investment itself changes the psychological frame of the event.

This project builds on these theoretical foundations to explore how ticket-purchasing decisions shape — and are shaped by — audience satisfaction in practice.

08

Expected Results

Based on existing literature and preliminary observation, several patterns are anticipated:

  • Age & Spending: Participants under 20 are expected to cluster in lower spending brackets ($30–50), while the 20–29 group — with more financial independence — may show higher willingness to spend on VIP or premium seats.
  • Seat vs. Satisfaction: VIP ticket holders are expected to report higher satisfaction scores (4–5), but the data may also reveal that budget-seat attendees report surprisingly high satisfaction when artist connection and atmosphere compensate for physical distance.
  • Key Purchase Driver: Artist popularity is hypothesized to be the dominant purchase factor — overriding price and seat location — consistent with fan culture research showing that loyalty to a specific performer is the primary motivator for concert attendance.
  • Frequent Attendees: Those who attend concerts often are predicted to prioritize experience quality (sound, atmosphere, stage production) over price, having developed clearer preferences over time.
  • Expectation Effect: Satisfaction may correlate less with spending and more with whether the event met or exceeded pre-concert expectations — a finding consistent with Pine & Gilmore's experience economy model.
09

Conclusion

Concert ticket purchasing is not a purely rational economic decision. It is a social, emotional, and cultural act — especially among younger audiences and dedicated fan communities. This research aims to surface the hidden logic behind those decisions and measure their downstream effect on satisfaction.

Understanding these dynamics has practical implications: concert organizers and venues can use audience satisfaction data to design better pricing tiers, improve seat category value, and create experiences that justify premium spending. For smaller or emerging artists, it may reveal that accessible pricing and strong stage presence deliver more satisfaction per dollar than elaborate production alone.

From a performance studies perspective, this research reinforces a core insight: the audience is not a passive recipient of performance. They are active co-creators of the experience, shaped by the choices they made before they ever entered the venue — including the choice of which ticket to buy, and how much to pay for it.
